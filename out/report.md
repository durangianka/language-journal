# Learner shorthand: Italian, Spanish, English

Scope: compact reference for recognizing meaningful informal language in a learner journal. Forms vary by region, age, platform, and relationship. They are context clues, not a universal dictionary.

## Italian

Examples confirmed in linguistic/reputable sources: **x / xké** = per / perché; **+** = più; **6** = sei; **cmq** = comunque; **nn** = non; **tt** = tutto; **anke** = anche; **ke** = che; **TVB** = ti voglio bene; **LOL**, **ASAP**, **IMHO** are international chat initialisms used in Italian digital writing. The requested **tt = tanto**, **xchè**, and **cia = ciao** were not confirmed in the checked sources. Prefer full words in formal writing. The University of Messina language advice treats SMS/chat abbreviations as suitable for informal contexts, not school essays or non-confidential/formal messages (Ruggiano, 2019).

Sources: Treccani, “Il linguaggio degli SMS” (2009), https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/il-linguaggio-degli-sms_%28XXI-Secolo%29/ ; Università di Messina, DICO “Abbreviazioni” (2019), https://dico.unime.it/ufaq/abbreviazioni/ ; Zanichelli, “Il linguaggio degli sms in Italia” (2008), https://aulalingue.scuola.zanichelli.it/benvenuti/2008/03/07/il-linguaggio-degli-sms-in-italia/ .

## Spanish

Examples: **xq / pq** = porque or por qué (syntax disambiguates); **tb** = también; **q** = que; **x** = por; **xo** = pero in one Mexican corpus, but can also be “yo” in another use; **xfa** = por favor; **salu2** = saludos; **bienv2** = bienvenidos. The Mexican university SMS study describes context as necessary for resolving abbreviated forms. **nse** = no sé and **sin emb** = sin embargo are **UNVERIFIED** in the academic/reputable sources checked; avoid treating them as stable forms without more regional evidence.

Sources: Hyejeong Jeong, “Mensajes de texto de celular en la comunicación de los universitarios mexicanos,” *Estudios de Lingüística Aplicada* (2013), https://www.uv.es/canea/archivos/Estudios_linguistica_2013.pdf ; Instituto Cervantes, Cervera Rodríguez, “La irrupción del coloquialismo en Internet y las nuevas tecnologías” (2001), https://cvc.cervantes.es/obref/congresos/valladolid/ponencias/nuevas_fronteras_del_espanol/4_lengua_y_escritura/cervera_a.htm .

## Register by learner level

CEFR gives proficiency descriptors for writing tasks and communicative competence, but does not say that shorthand is expected at a particular level. The table below is an **app policy inference**, informed by CEFR writing progression and advice to match register to audience/task. Apply it only when the learner’s task is informal journaling; correct shorthand gently by showing a standard form, without rejecting understandable language.

| App level | Suggested handling of meaningful shorthand | Rationale / limit |
|---|---|---|
| New (pre-A1/A1) | Tolerate a few recognizable forms; show the expanded form as a model. | Early writing focuses on familiar words, phrases, and simple messages. Do not require slang knowledge. |
| Basic (A2) | Tolerate familiar chat forms in casual journal entries; correct or explain forms that obscure a target structure. | Learners can write short routine notes and messages; register remains task dependent. |
| Intermediate (B1/B2) | Tolerate in explicitly informal entries; suggest standard spelling when it supports accuracy or transfer. | Connected writing and some register awareness are developing; abbreviations are not themselves proof of weak language. |
| Advanced (C1/C2) | Tolerate if purposeful and contextually apt; correct for formal or standard-writing tasks. | Greater ability to adapt register is expected, but informal shorthand remains a valid register choice. |

Sources: Council of Europe, *CEFR Companion Volume* (2020), https://rm.coe.int/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages-learning-teaching/16809ea0d4 ; Council of Europe CEFR descriptors page (updated descriptor resource, accessed 2026), https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/cefr-descriptors ; University of Messina DICO (2019), above. Exact level-by-level shorthand rules: **UNVERIFIED / not specified by CEFR**.

## Detection clues

**Likely meaningful language with shorthand:** one or more ordinary words remain; a candidate expansion fits grammar and nearby context; the same token maps consistently in a sentence; conversational intent is recoverable; punctuation, accents, or vowels are selectively dropped; symbols/numbers have a known local reading (Italian x = per, Spanish salu2 = saludos, English gr8 = great). Ambiguous forms require context, never a single-token decision.

**Likely no language meaning:** only formulas/operators, binary/hex/Morse-like sequences, or long digit strings with no linguistic anchors; isolated random letters with no plausible expansion; code syntax or pasted source code; repeated-character noise; no coherent words or recoverable message after conservative expansion. These are heuristic signals. A valid numeric date, math explanation, code-switch, name, or beginner spelling should not be refused solely because it contains digits or unusual tokens.

Years above are publication years where displayed by the source; access year is shown for undated live dictionary/CEFR pages.
